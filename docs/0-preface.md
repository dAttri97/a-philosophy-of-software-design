# Chapter 0 Preface

Human beings have been writing programs for electronic computers for more than 80 years, yet it is surprising how little discussion there has been about how to design programs or what good programs should look like. There has been considerable discussion of software development processes (such as agile development) and development tools (such as debuggers, version-control systems, and test-coverage tools). Programming techniques (such as object-oriented and functional programming), design patterns, and algorithms have also been analyzed thoroughly. All of these discussions are valuable, but they barely touch the core questions of software design. David Parnas’s classic paper “On the Criteria To Be Used in Decomposing Systems into Modules” was published in 1971, yet in the 45 years that followed, progress in software design practice has not greatly surpassed that paper.

The most fundamental problem in computer science is problem decomposition: how to break a complex problem into smaller pieces that can be solved independently. Decomposition is the main design work programmers face every day, yet I have not found a single university course anywhere that takes decomposition as its central theme, apart from the work described here. We teach loops and object-oriented programming, but not software design.

Moreover, programmers differ enormously in quality and productivity, but we rarely study what makes excellent programmers excellent or how to teach those skills in our classrooms. I have spoken with several programmers I consider outstanding, but most of them find it hard to articulate what specific techniques give them their edge. Many people believe software design skill is innate talent that cannot be taught. Yet there is considerable scientific evidence that outstanding performance in many fields owes more to high-quality practice than to innate ability (see, for example, Geoff Colvin’s *Talent Is Overrated*).

These questions have puzzled and frustrated me for years. I wondered whether software design could be taught and assumed that design skill is what separates great programmers from average ones. I eventually concluded that the only way to answer these questions was to try teaching a course on software design. That course is Stanford’s CS 190. In it I present a set of principles for software design. Students then work through a series of projects to understand and apply those principles. The course is run like a traditional writing class: in English class students use an iterative process (draft, feedback, rewrite) to improve. In CS 190 students build a real piece of software from scratch. We then use thorough code reviews to surface design problems. Students revise their projects to fix those problems. In this way they can see how applying design principles improves their code.

I have now taught this software design course many times, and this book is based on the design principles that emerged in the classroom. Those principles are fairly high-level and border on philosophy (“define errors out of existence”), which makes them hard for students to grasp in the abstract. Students learn best by writing code, making mistakes, understanding why they erred, and then fixing their code in light of the principles.

You may be wondering: what makes me think I have all the answers about software design? Frankly, I don’t. When I learned to program there were no courses on software design, and I never had a mentor who taught me design principles. In my student days code review barely existed. My ideas about software design come from personal experience writing and reading code. Over my career I have written roughly 250,000 lines of code in several languages. I have worked on multiple teams, created three operating systems from scratch, several file and storage systems, infrastructure tools such as debuggers and build systems, a GUI toolkit, a scripting language, and interactive editors for text, drawing, presentations, and integrated circuits. Along the way I have lived through the problems of large systems and tried many design techniques. I have also read a great deal of code written by others, which exposed me to a wide range of approaches, good and bad.

From all of this experience I have tried to extract common themes: mistakes to avoid and techniques that work. This book reflects that practice: every problem described here is one I have personally encountered, and every recommended technique is one I have applied successfully in my own coding.

I do not intend this book to be the last word on software design; I am sure I will miss valuable techniques, and in the long run some of my advice may turn out to be bad advice. Even so, I hope this book can start a conversation about software design. Compare the ideas here with your own experience and judge for yourself whether the approaches described here really reduce complexity. The book is a collection of opinions, so some readers will disagree with some of my suggestions. If you disagree, try to understand why. I am very interested in what works and what does not for you, and in any other thoughts you have about software design. I hope the discussion that follows will raise our collective level of software design skill. I will fold what I learn into future editions of the book. I suggest you read this book with a healthy skepticism. The overall goal is to reduce complexity; that matters more than any particular principle or idea in these pages. If you try an idea from the book and find that it does not actually reduce complexity, you need not keep using it (but please tell me your experience—I want feedback on what works and what does not).

The quality of the book has benefited from criticism and suggestions.
